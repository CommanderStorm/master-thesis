\section{Results}\label{sec:results}
After describing the experimental setup (\cref{sec:results:setup}), we report results in the order of the pipeline.
The style-driven passes alone (\cref{sec:results:style}), tile shaving and adaptive \ac{MLT} encoding (\cref{sec:results:data}), and finally the joint pipeline and whether its style-level and data-level passes are additive or synergistic (\cref{sec:results:combined}), before assessing generalization to unseen styles (\cref{sec:results:generalization}).

\subsection{Experimental Setup and Corpora}\label{sec:results:setup}
We evaluate the pipeline across three tiers of breadth.
A benchmarking corpus of \num{10} publicly available MapLibre styles for the \ac{OMT} schema, four from OpenFreeMap (Liberty, Bright, Positron, Fiord) and six from the \ac{OMT} community (Dark~Matter, OSM~Bright, Klokan-Basic, Toner, OSM~Liberty, Stadia~Outdoors), spanning compact basemaps of under \num{50} layers to styles with over \num{100}, is rendered end-to-end against the Germany \ac{OMT} tileset (zoom levels 0 to 14) under \num{18} scenarios, each a pairing of a geographic location with a camera animation, selected so that every location appears at least once and dense and sparse, Latin and non-Latin cases are all represented.
The same \num{10} styles are also evaluated at the tile-shaving size level on the same tileset, and a third generalization corpus of \num{12} additional unseen styles drawn from the Maputnik gallery is evaluated with static analysis only.
The measurement harness is a Puppeteer-driven headless browser that loads MapLibre GL JS, replays each animation, and collects load-, first tile and per-frame-times through the browser \texttt{Performance} API under Vulkan based rendering.
Each style and scenario cell is run \num{15} times to account for per-run variance.
The pipeline itself is measured through a 19-step cumulative ablation in which each pass is added on top of all preceding passes, plus one plain-tile-shaving comparison configuration.

\subsection{Expression- and Structural-Level Style Passes}\label{sec:results:style}
The eight expression-level passes barely moved end-to-end load time on their own, reducing the per-style median by \qty{1.6}{\percent} (range \qtyrange{-7.3}{+12.3}{\percent} across styles), an effect not consistently distinguishable from per-style measurement noise.
Their effect on serialized style size was clearer.
Default stripping was the single largest marginal contributor at \qty{-3.4}{\percent} of raw style size (\qty{-2.5}{\percent} under gzip), followed by stats-driven folding (\qty{-1.5}{\percent}) and expression simplification (\qty{-1.1}{\percent}).
Raw style size fell by up to \qty{20.3}{\percent} on Fiord (\num{22.2} to \qty{17.7}{\kilo\byte}) and \qty{7.0}{\percent} on Liberty, narrowing to \qty{8.3}{\percent} and \qty{5.9}{\percent} respectively under gzip as the compressor absorbed part of the raw savings.
The size reduction translated directly into a shorter style-parse step even where end-to-end load time was unchanged.

The structural passes concentrated almost all of their gains in dead-layer elimination and layer merging, with the remaining passes negligible in isolation.
The magnitude was style-dependent.
Fiord's 48-layer style shed two layers (\qty{-4.2}{\percent}, \qty{-14.1}{\percent} gzip transfer, \qty{-36.8}{\percent} expression-tree nodes from \num{1610} to \num{1018}), while Liberty's 111-layer style shed four in the layer-merge step (\qty{-3.6}{\percent}, nodes from \num{3537} to \num{3167}), bringing its cumulative style-level reduction to \qty{-4.9}{\percent} gzip.
Layer merging moves raw byte count in either direction (\qty{-372}{\byte} on Liberty, \qty[print-implicit-plus = true]{+201}{\byte} on Bright) because a merged layer replaces several short filters with wordier \mlop{match}-keyed expressions.
Compressed size grows slightly in both cases (\qty[print-implicit-plus = true]{+36}{\byte} and \qty[print-implicit-plus = true]{+47}{\byte} gzip) because the merged expressions compress less well than the repetitive originals they replace.
The step still pays off at render time, cutting per-frame draw calls and raising FPS on both styles (Liberty \num{642} to \num{664}, Bright \num{621} to \num{632}).
Symbol-heavy styles benefit less because symbol layers are excluded from merging on collision-soundness grounds.

\subsection{Style-Driven Tile Shaving and Adaptive \acs{MLT} Encoding}\label{sec:results:data}
Tile shaving dominates the improvements.
Pooled across the \num{10} styles, shaving alone cuts the median load time from \qty{390}{\milli\second} to \qty{135}{\milli\second} (\qty{-65.5}{\percent}) and roughly doubled median FPS (\num{2.1}$\times$), whereas style-only optimization contributed a modest \qty{-4.0}{\percent} (\cref{fig:box}).
Across the \num{10} styles, the transfer-size reduction from style-driven shaving on the Germany tileset ranged from \qty{19.8}{\percent} (Bright) to \qty{46.3}{\percent} (Toner) with a median of \qty{28.6}{\percent} (\cref{fig:tile_shave_per_style}), the spread reflecting how many source-layers and property columns each style references.

Our \ac{MLT} encoder alone did not have a large impact on performance, but noticably impacted transfer size.
\Cref{tab:encoder} reports encoded size and full-decode latency for the Germany \ac{OMT} tileset under three encoders.
Once both are gzipped, the reference \ac{MLT} encoder is larger than \ac{MVT}, because general-purpose compressors already exploit most of the row-level redundancy a fixed-plan columnar encoder cannot match.
Adding sort-strategy competition, per-stream profiling, and shared-dictionary clustering reverses this.
Our encoder falls below \ac{MVT}+gzip even uncompressed and stays \qty{12.3}{\percent} smaller once both are gzipped, holding its edge even against zstd-22, the strongest general-purpose compressor tested.
Our native Rust decoder is \numrange{4.6}{8.3}$\times$ faster than \ac{MVT}+gzip across zoom levels 4, 7, and 13, but this advantage does not reach the browser load-time numbers, which use the existing JavaScript MLT decoder rather than the Rust path.
Decomposing the transfer-size savings by zoom level (\cref{fig:shaving}) separates the two effects.
The gap between each format and its shaved counterpart is removed via the pruning advisory.
This dominates at the dense mid-zoom levels. The remaining gap between shaved \ac{MVT}/\ac{MLT} is format dependent.

\subsection{Combined Pipeline: Additive or Synergistic?}\label{sec:results:combined}
Whether the style-level passes (expression and structural) and the data-level passes (shaving and encoding) interact synergistically depends on the metric and the style.
On data volume they are additive for every style.
Shaving driven by the optimized style removed essentially the same bytes as shaving driven by the unoptimized style: deriving the pruning advisory from the optimized rather than the original style raised the tile-data reduction by only \qtyrange{0.0}{0.5}{\percent} of the baseline volume (median \qty{0.08}{\percent}).
The advisory depends only on which source-layers and property columns a style references, which simplification leaves intact.

At load time the two groups are additive in aggregate.
Pooled across the \num{10} styles, the combined reduction (\qty{66.4}{\percent}) matches the sum of the style-only (\qty{4.0}{\percent}) and shaving-only (\qty{65.5}{\percent}) reductions to within a \qty{-3.1}{\percent} interaction term.
Per scenario, small super-additive interactions are common.
Combined load time beat the sum of the parts in \num{103} of \num{180} style-scenario pairs (\qty{57}{\percent}).
They split the corpus in half: five styles synergize in \numrange{13}{15} of their \num{18} scenarios (Bright at a median of \qty{+7.1}{\percent}), while the other five do so in only \numrange{4}{8}, independently of how much each style shaves.
Toner, which shaves the most, stays additive, while OSM~Bright, which shaves the least, synergizes the most.
The interactions are small (median \qty{+0.64}{\percent} across all pairs) and offset one another, so they do not lift the aggregate.
Because style optimization leaves tile volume unchanged, this residual synergy is a second-order rendering and decode timing effect, not a data-volume effect.
Style-level passes therefore act as enablers that simplify the advisory, and for some styles modestly improve render timing, rather than as co-equal synergistic partners.

The ablation confirms this (\cref{fig:box,fig:waterfall}).
Load-time variance compresses only when tile shaving fires, the FPS spread widens past the same step, and almost all of the style-size win lands by metadata stripping.
The load-time budget breakdown (\cref{fig:time_breakdown}) shows the same split.
Style-parse time falls with each expression pass, from roughly \qty{1.8}{\milli\second} to \qty{0.2}{\milli\second}.
At under \qty{2}{\milli\second} it is negligible against the loading time of several hundred milliseconds, so we fold it into the first-frame leg rather than plot it separately.
The first-tile and remaining-load legs move only once tile shaving fires.
Composed end-to-end across the \num{10} styles, the full pipeline cut load time by \qtyrange{63}{74}{\percent} (median \qty{71}{\percent}) and raised FPS to \numrange{2.0}{3.0}$\times$ (median \num{2.5}$\times$).
Browser heap fell \qty{35.5}{\percent}, from \qty{84.2}{\mega\byte} to \qty{54.3}{\mega\byte}, almost entirely from the smaller in-memory tile payload after shaving (\cref{fig:memory_ablation}).
Bootstrap \qty{95}{\percent} confidence intervals confirmed the full-pipeline load-time reductions were tight (Fiord \qty{370.9}{\milli\second} to \qty{120.5}{\milli\second}, Liberty \qty{466.5}{\milli\second} to \qty{133.7}{\milli\second}, both $p < 10^{-45}$, Wilcoxon).

\subsection{Cross-Style Generalization}\label{sec:results:generalization}
Applied with static passes only to the \num{12} unseen Maputnik styles, the optimizer delivered a median raw style-size reduction of \qty{30.8}{\percent} (range \qtyrange{3.2}{67.7}{\percent}), narrowing to a median of \qty{10.8}{\percent} under gzip (range \qtyrange{3.9}{22.4}{\percent}), with no style regressing (\cref{fig:cross_style}).
The top reducers were styles with verbose filter logic or repetitive property structures (LocationIQ Streets at \qty{67.7}{\percent} and MapTiler Toner at \qty{57.5}{\percent}), while already-minified basemaps such as Stadia Outdoors (\qty{3.2}{\percent}) and the AWS pair (\qty{10.6}{\percent}) left little to remove.
Initial style complexity did not reliably predict achievable reduction (Pearson $r = -0.54$, $n = 12$, $p = 0.07$).
Because the generalization corpus carries no tile data, stats-driven and data-level passes were not applied, so these figures are a lower bound on the full potential.
