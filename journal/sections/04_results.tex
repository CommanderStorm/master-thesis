\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Experimental setup and corpora}\label{sec:results:setup}
We evaluate the pipeline across three tiers of breadth.
A benchmarking corpus of 10 MapLibre styles is rendered end-to-end against the Germany OpenMapTiles (OMT) tileset (zoom levels 0 to 14, \qty{256.5}{\kilo tiles}) under \num{18} scenarios, each a pairing of a geographic location with a camera animation, selected so that every location appears at least once and dense and sparse, Latin and non-Latin cases are all represented.
The same \num{10} styles are also evaluated at the tile-shaving size level on the same tileset, and a third generalization corpus of \num{12} additional unseen styles drawn from the Maputnik gallery is evaluated with static analysis only.
The measurement harness is a Puppeteer-driven headless browser that loads MapLibre GL JS, replays each animation, and collects load time, frame-times, and time to first tile through the browser \texttt{Performance} API under Vulkan based rendering.
Each style and scenario cell is run \num{15} times and reported as the median.
The pipeline itself is measured through a 19-step cumulative ablation in which each pass is added on top of all preceding passes, plus one plain-tile-shaving comparison configuration.

\subsection{Style-level optimization}\label{sec:results:style}
The eight expression-level passes barely moved end-to-end load time on their own, reducing the per-style median by \qty{1.6}{\percent} (range \qtyrange{-7.3}{+12.3}{\percent} across styles), an effect not consistently distinguishable from per-style measurement noise.
Their effect on serialized style size was clearer: default stripping was the single largest marginal contributor at \qty{-3.4}{\percent} of raw style size (\qty{-2.5}{\percent} under gzip), followed by stats-driven folding (\qty{-1.5}{\percent}) and expression simplification (\qty{-1.1}{\percent}).
Raw style size fell by up to \qty{20.3}{\percent} on Fiord (\num{22.2} to \qty{17.7}{\kilo\byte}) and \qty{7.0}{\percent} on Liberty, narrowing to \qty{8.3}{\percent} and \qty{5.9}{\percent} respectively under gzip as the compressor absorbed part of the raw savings.
The size reduction translated directly into a shorter style-parse step even where end-to-end load time was unchanged.

The structural passes concentrated almost all of their gains in dead-layer elimination and layer merging, with the remaining passes negligible in isolation.
The magnitude was style-dependent: Fiord's 48-layer style shed two layers (\qty{-4.2}{\percent}, \qty{-14.1}{\percent} gzip transfer, \qty{-36.8}{\percent} expression-tree nodes from \num{1610} to \num{1018}), while Liberty's 111-layer style shed four through layer merging (\qty{-3.6}{\percent}, \qty{-4.9}{\percent} gzip, nodes from \num{3537} to \num{3167}).
Layer merging can increase raw byte count, because a merged layer replaces several short filters with wordier \mlop{match}-keyed expressions, yet still reduces per-frame draw calls and frame time.
Gzip absorbs the raw increase, leaving compressed size essentially unchanged.
Symbol-heavy styles benefit less because symbol layers are excluded from merging on collision-soundness grounds.

\subsection{Tile shaving and columnar re-encoding}\label{sec:results:data}
Tile shaving dominates the improvements.
Pooled across the \num{10} deep-corpus styles, shaving alone cut the median load time from \qty{390}{\milli\second} to \qty{135}{\milli\second} (\qty{-65.5}{\percent}) and roughly doubled median FPS (\num{2.1}$\times$), whereas style-only optimization contributed a modest \qty{-4.0}{\percent} (Figure~\ref{fig:box}).
Across the \num{10} styles with available tile data, style-driven on-wire reductions on the Germany tileset ranged from \qty{19.8}{\percent} (Bright) to \qty{46.3}{\percent} (Toner) with a median of \qty{28.6}{\percent} (Figure~\ref{fig:tile_shave_per_style}), the spread reflecting how many source-layers and property columns each style references.

Our MLT encoder alone did not have a large impact on performance, but noticably impacted transfer size.
Table~\ref{tab:encoder} reports encoded size and full-decode latency for the Germany OMT tileset under three encoders.
Once both are gzipped, the reference MLT encoder is larger than MVT, because general-purpose compressors already exploit most of the row-level redundancy a fixed-plan columnar encoder cannot match.
Adding sort-strategy competition, per-stream profiling, and shared-dictionary clustering reverses this.
Our encoder falls below MVT+gzip even uncompressed and stays \qty{12.3}{\percent} smaller once both are gzipped, holding its edge even against zstd-22, the strongest general-purpose compressor tested.
Our native Rust decoder is \numrange{4.6}{8.3}$\times$ faster than MVT+gzip across zoom levels 4, 7, and 13, but this advantage does not reach the browser load-time numbers, which use the existing JavaScript MLT decoder rather than the Rust path.
Decomposing the on-wire savings by zoom level (Figure~\ref{fig:shaving}) separates the two effects.
The gap between each format and its shaved counterpart is the payload removed by the pruning advisory, which dominates at the dense mid-zoom levels, while the residual gap between shaved MVT and shaved MLT is the additional contribution of columnar encoding.

\subsection{Combined pipeline: additive or synergistic?}\label{sec:results:combined}
Whether the two levels interact synergistically depends on the metric and the style.
On data volume they are additive for every style. Shaving the optimized style removed essentially the same bytes as shaving the unoptimized one, the optimized advisory adding \qtyrange{0.0}{0.5}{\percent} (median \qty{0.08}{\percent}).
The advisory depends only on which source-layers and property columns a style references, which simplification leaves intact.

On load time the levels are additive in aggregate. Pooled across the \num{10} styles, the combined reduction (\qty{66.4}{\percent}) matches the sum of the style-only (\qty{4.0}{\percent}) and shaving-only (\qty{65.5}{\percent}) reductions to within a \qty{-3.1}{\percent} interaction term.
Per scenario, small super-additive interactions are common. Combined load time beat the sum of the parts in \num{103} of \num{180} style-scenario pairs (\qty{57}{\percent}).
They concentrate in a few styles (Bright \num{15} of \num{18} pairs, median \qty{+7.1}{\percent}; OSM~Bright and OSM~Liberty \num{15} of \num{18}; Stadia~Outdoors \num{14}; Liberty \num{13}) and are largely absent in others (Fiord \num{4} of \num{18}, Positron \num{5}, Dark~Matter \num{6}, Klokan-Basic \num{8}, Toner \num{8}), independently of how much each style shaves. Toner shaves the most yet stays additive, while OSM~Bright shaves the least yet synergizes the most.
The interactions are small (median \qty{+0.64}{\percent} across all pairs) and offset one another, so they do not lift the aggregate.
Because style optimization leaves tile volume unchanged, this residual synergy is a second-order rendering and decode timing effect, not a data-volume effect.
Style-level passes therefore act as enablers that simplify the advisory, and for some styles modestly improve render timing, rather than as co-equal synergistic partners.

The 19-step ablation confirms this (Figures~\ref{fig:box} and~\ref{fig:waterfall}). Load-time variance compresses only when tile shaving fires, the FPS spread widens past the same step, and almost all of the style-size win lands by metadata stripping.
The load-time budget breakdown (Figure~\ref{fig:time_breakdown}) shows the same split. Style-parse time falls with each expression pass, from roughly \qty{1.8}{\milli\second} to \qty{0.2}{\milli\second}, but at under \qty{2}{\milli\second} it is negligible against a load budget of several hundred milliseconds, so we fold it into the first-frame leg rather than plot it separately. The first-tile and remaining-load legs move only once tile shaving fires.
Composed end-to-end across the \num{10} deep-corpus styles, the full pipeline cut load time by \qtyrange{63}{74}{\percent} (median \qty{71}{\percent}) and raised FPS to \numrange{2.0}{3.0}$\times$ (median \num{2.5}$\times$).
Browser heap fell \qty{35.5}{\percent}, from \qty{84.2}{\mega\byte} to \qty{54.3}{\mega\byte}, almost entirely from the smaller in-memory tile payload after shaving (Figure~\ref{fig:memory_ablation}).
Bootstrap \qty{95}{\percent} confidence intervals confirmed the full-pipeline load-time reductions were tight (Fiord \qty{370.9}{\milli\second} to \qty{120.5}{\milli\second}, Liberty \qty{466.5}{\milli\second} to \qty{133.7}{\milli\second}, both $p < 10^{-45}$, Wilcoxon).

\subsection{Cross-style generalization}\label{sec:results:generalization}
Applied with static passes only to the \num{12} unseen Maputnik styles, the optimizer delivered a median raw style-size reduction of \qty{30.8}{\percent} (range \qtyrange{3.2}{67.7}{\percent}), narrowing to a median of \qty{10.8}{\percent} under gzip (range \qtyrange{3.9}{22.4}{\percent}), with no style regressing (Figure~\ref{fig:cross_style}).
The top reducers were styles with verbose filter logic or repetitive property structures (LocationIQ Streets at \qty{67.7}{\percent} and MapTiler Toner at \qty{57.5}{\percent}), while already-minified basemaps such as Stadia Outdoors (\qty{3.2}{\percent}) and the AWS pair (\qty{10.6}{\percent}) left little to remove.
Initial style complexity did not reliably predict achievable reduction (Pearson $r = -0.54$, $n = 12$, $p = 0.07$).
Because the generalization corpus carries no tile data, stats-driven and data-level passes were not applied, so these figures are a lower bound on the full potential.

\subsection{Threats to validity}\label{sec:results:threats}
All rendering benchmarks ran on a single hardware and browser configuration with ANGLE/Vulkan, so results may differ on other hardware or graphics backends, and the 15-run median may not capture rare events such as thermal throttling.
The evaluation is limited to MapLibre GL JS and the OMT schema, and the 10 benchmark styles, though diverse, may not cover all authoring patterns.
Network conditions were simulated through a fixed bandwidth throttle and a caching proxy rather than real content delivery network latency, which also masks real-world cache behavior.
The cumulative ablation design means each pass's reported marginal contribution depends on the order of preceding passes, and tail metrics (p99 frame time, jank count) carry higher variance than central-tendency metrics.
Because the \num{18} scenarios share hardware, tile data, and styles, they are not fully independent, so we read the reported $p$-values as descriptive evidence of large effect sizes rather than strict inferential tests, and apply no multiple-comparisons correction across the full ablation grid.
Visual-equivalence checks across the corpus, all scenarios, and zoom levels 0 to 14 confirmed every pass stays within the per-test pixel-difference tolerance of Section~\ref{sec:methods:problem}.
